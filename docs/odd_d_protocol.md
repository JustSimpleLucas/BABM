## Part A — Full ODD+D description

## I. Overview

### I.i Purpose

#### I.i.a What is the purpose of the study?

BABM asks whether a stock market of heterogeneous, boundedly rational investors,
whose decisions embed behavioural-finance mechanisms, generates the stylised facts
of financial returns. It asks this particularly for stress and crisis regimes. The
targeted facts are:

- fat tails and excess kurtosis;
- the absence of linear autocorrelation in raw returns;
- volatility clustering (Cont, 2001).

The behavioural mechanisms are:

1. **Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT) valuation.** Reference dependence, loss
   aversion, diminishing sensitivity and probability weighting (Kahneman & Tversky,
   1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992).
2. **Social influence.** Investors exchange opinions with neighbours in a social
   network.
3. **Heterogeneous expectations.** Fundamentalists, four kinds of technical
   (chartist) rule, noise traders, and passive investors.
4. **Institutional frictions.** Short-sale margin and borrowing limits, forced
   buy-ins, proportional transaction costs, and a price tick.

No pricing equation sets the price. Each asset is traded in its own persistent
continuous double-auction limit-order book (LOB), and the official price is the
last transaction price. Every statistical property of simulated returns is
therefore generated by order flow.

In ODD+D's categories the study serves three purposes:

- **Theory development and system understanding.** The model is a generative test
  of whether these micro-level mechanisms are sufficient to produce the macro-level
  patterns. Each mechanism can be switched off, so its necessity can be tested by
  controlled contrast. The run identifier records the principal contrasts: CPT
  on/off, CPT preference heterogeneity on/off, social influence on/off, short
  selling on/off, and the population mix.
- **Quantitative comparison with data.** Simulated returns are calibrated and
  validated against daily returns of the S&P 500, NASDAQ-100 and FTSE 100 indices
  and of VinFast Auto equity. Several stylised facts are used jointly to accept or
  reject mechanisms, as in pattern-oriented modelling (Grimm et al., 2005).
- **An individual case study.** An optional *protagonist* — one investor with a
  stated endowment, trading rule and CPT preferences — is followed through the run.
  This asks what happens to one identifiable investor in the market the population
  generates.

#### I.i.b For whom is the model designed?

The model is designed for scientists. The primary readers are the PhD supervisory
team, examiners, and researchers in behavioural finance, agent-based computational
finance and market microstructure who need to assess, replicate or extend it. The
formal specification and the single configuration file let a reader trace every
modelling assumption to code.

A secondary audience is regulators and policy analysts, because the model contains
levers they set: short-sale permission, margin requirements, borrowable supply,
transaction costs, tick size, and a market-order price collar. The model has not
been validated for decision support. It is not participatory, and it is not built
for teaching.

**Global state.** Time $t$; the cross-asset fundamental correlation
$\rho_{AB}$; the shared rolling covariance matrix $\Sigma_t$; the risk-free
rate $r_f$; the microstructure parameters.

**Units.** Currency is an arbitrary numeraire, with prices normalised to 100 at
$t=0$. Quantities are shares and may be fractional (the minimum order quantity is
$10^{-8}$). Returns are per-step log returns. Social distances are dimensionless.
Time is counted in steps.

#### I.ii.c What are the exogenous factors / drivers of the model?

1. **Fundamental-value innovations.** Each asset's latent fundamental value follows
   a geometric Brownian motion with constant drift and volatility. Innovations are
   equicorrelated across assets with correlation $\rho_{AB}$. This is the model's
   only source of exogenous "news". It has no jumps, regimes or scheduled shocks.
   It is drawn from its own random stream, so changes to agent behaviour never alter
   the fundamental path.
2. **The risk-free rate.** Constant; 0 in the reference configuration.
3. **Institutional settings.** Whether short selling is permitted, the margin
   requirements, borrowable supply, transaction-cost rate, tick size, market-order
   collar and order expiry. These are fixed scenario parameters within a run.

Crises are not imposed. Stress and crisis regimes are treated as endogenous
outcomes and identified after the run. The same rule is applied to simulated and
empirical series: a step is in a stress regime when its rolling 30-step volatility
is in the top quintile of the series, or when the drawdown reaches 10%
(`statistics/stress.py`). Stress-prone conditions are studied by varying parameters
— fundamental volatility, correlation, short-sale and margin rules, loss aversion,
social influence — rather than by injecting events.

Noise-trader beliefs and private signals are stochastic, but they belong to the
agents (II.ix) rather than to the environment. No external time series enters a
simulation (III.iii).

#### I.ii.d If applicable, how is space included in the model?

Space is not geographic. Agents occupy fixed positions in a synthetic, continuous
two-dimensional social space: the unit square $[0,1]\times[0,1]$, without
periodic boundaries. The space is used only to decide who influences whom. Agents
within the interaction radius $r_{\mathrm{soc}}$ are neighbours, and they weight one
another by $1/(1+d)$, where $d$ is their distance.

The space is therefore an abstract device for generating a random geometric graph
(Penrose, 2003), not a representation of location. Markets and order books are
aspatial: any agent can trade with any other.

#### I.ii.e What are the temporal and spatial resolutions and extents of the model?

- **Time.** Discrete steps; one step is one trading period. The model does not fix
  a calendar length. When simulated returns are compared with daily benchmark
  returns in calibration and validation, a step is in effect read as one trading
  day (Part C, point 1).
- **Within a step.** Orders are matched one at a time in random arrival order, as
  a continuous double auction. The official price is recorded once per step (the
  last transaction price), so every output is per step.
- **Extent.** A warm-up of $T_w=25$ steps builds the price history that indicators
  and the covariance estimate need; it is not analysed. It is followed by $T$
  analysed steps. $T=100$ in the reference demonstration file; analyses use longer
  runs (the regression baseline in `docs/baselines.md` uses 50,000 steps). $T$ is
  set per experiment.
- **Social space.** The unit square with $r_{\mathrm{soc}}=0.4$ gives a dense
  network. In the reference run the mean degree is 102 of 300 possible neighbours
  (minimum 35, maximum 155), a density of 0.34. This matches the analytic value
  $\pi r^2-\tfrac83 r^3+\tfrac12 r^4\approx0.345$ for uniformly placed points.
- **Price grid.** Tick $\delta=0.01$ on prices initialised at 100.
- **Empirical comparators.** About 2,270 daily returns per index (2017–2026) and
  967 for VinFast (2021–2026).

### I.iii Process overview and scheduling

#### I.iii.a What entity does what, and in what order?

Source: `src/Simulation/SimulationEngine.cpp`, formalism §2, and
`docs/simulation_flowchart.mmd`.

```text
INITIALISATION
  validate and normalise the configuration
      (disabled assets dropped; short-exposure caps forced to 0 when short selling is off)
  build the population
      shuffle strategy labels before assigning ids; sample each chartist's rule;
      draw CPT parameters; select CPT users on a separate stream;
      add the protagonist last, consuming no shared random draws
  social model: draw a position, gamma_i and sigma_priv_i for every agent;
                build the neighbour graph if social influence is enabled
  market: P_j0 = F_j0 = initial price; run T_w warm-up steps of a fundamental-pull price process
  agents: value initial wealth and entry prices at the last warm-up price

FOR t = 1 .. T
   1  every agent's cash accrues at r_f                                     [all agents]
   2  all fundamentals advance in one correlated draw                        [exogenous]
   3  opinions update from neighbours' lagged opinions and private signals   [all agents, synchronous]
   4  each book expires old resting orders and cancels stale ones at random  [per asset]
   5  draw a random permutation of agents; each stays active with prob. alpha_act
   6  estimate the shared covariance Sigma_t from trailing log returns       [global]
   7  in permutation order, each active agent generates at most one order per asset
      from the start-of-step state; each order is tagged MARKET or LIMIT
   8  in the same order, each order is matched against its own asset's book
      (price-time priority, self-trade prevention, collar, feasibility rechecked at execution);
      limit residuals rest; cash, holdings, entry prices, costs and the ledger update at each fill
   9  each asset: P_{j,t+1} = last trade price, otherwise the previous price
  10  refresh wealth and peak wealth; enforce maintenance margin by forced buy-ins
      (short selling only); refresh wealth again if a buy-in traded
  11  record per-step diagnostics
END
export artefacts, screening metrics and the experiment manifest
```

**Synchronous and asynchronous updating.**

- The fundamental update and the opinion update are synchronous. Opinions are
  computed from lagged values only, through a scratch buffer.
- Decisions use one start-of-step information set: prices, $\Sigma_t$ and the
  agent's own portfolio. All orders are generated before any of them executes, so
  no agent reacts within a step to another agent's trades.
- Execution is asynchronous (random sequential). Orders reach the books one at a
  time in the random activation order. An order can trade against orders resting
  from earlier steps or placed earlier in the same step. A fresh permutation each
  step prevents a persistent first-mover advantage; updating schemes are known to
  affect agent-based results (Huberman & Glance, 1993; Axtell, 2001).
- One agent's orders across assets come from a single joint portfolio solve. A
  running cash budget stops simultaneous buys in several assets from committing the
  same cash twice.

## II. Design concepts

### II.i Theoretical and empirical background

#### II.i.a Which general concepts, theories or hypotheses underlie the model's design (apart from the decision model)? What is the link to complexity and to the purpose?

- **Heterogeneous-agent models of asset markets.** Interaction between
  fundamentalists and chartists can generate excess volatility, bubbles and
  crashes, fat tails and volatility clustering (Frankel & Froot, 1990; Brock &
  Hommes, 1998; Lux & Marchesi, 1999; Hommes, 2006; LeBaron, 2006). BABM keeps
  this ecology but forms prices in an order book instead of through a market-maker
  equation.
- **Noise-trader risk and limits to arbitrage.** Traders with stochastic beliefs
  can push prices away from fundamentals when arbitrage is risky or constrained
  (Black, 1986; De Long et al., 1990; Shleifer & Vishny, 1997).
- **Short-sale constraints.** Constraints slow the incorporation of pessimistic
  opinion into prices (Miller, 1977; Diamond & Verrecchia, 1987). This is why
  short selling, margin and borrowable supply are explicit switches.
- **Market microstructure.** Agent-based order-book models show that the trading
  mechanism itself shapes return statistics (Raberto et al., 2001; Chiarella &
  Iori, 2002; Farmer, Patelli & Zovko, 2005; Chiarella, Iori & Perelló, 2009). The
  model also measures the allocative efficiency of its double auction, as
  experimental economics does (Smith, 1962; Gode & Sunder, 1993).
- **Fundamental value as geometric Brownian motion.** This is the standard model of
  log-normally evolving value (Black & Scholes, 1973). Its log-form discretisation
  is exact (Glasserman, 2004).
- **Herding and social influence.** The literature on imitation and information
  cascades (Banerjee, 1992; Kirman, 1993; Bikhchandani & Sharma, 2000) is
  represented by linear opinion pooling (DeGroot, 1974). Mixing that pooled opinion
  with a fresh private signal resembles the Friedkin & Johnsen (1990) model.

**Link to complexity and purpose.** Stylised facts are macro-level regularities
with no counterpart in any individual rule, so they are candidate emergent
properties of interaction through the order book and the social network. The model
tests whether specified micro mechanisms are sufficient to generate them. Using
several patterns jointly — the return distribution, linear dependence and
nonlinear dependence — to discriminate between mechanisms follows pattern-oriented
modelling (Grimm et al., 2005).

#### II.i.b On what assumptions are the agents' decision models based?

The decision models combine established theory with heuristics grounded in
observation.

- **Heuristic expectations (bounded rationality).** Agents forecast with simple
  rules — fundamental reversion and technical analysis — rather than rational
  expectations. Survey evidence shows market participants use both kinds of
  forecast (Frankel & Froot, 1990), and simple technical rules have measurable
  predictive content (Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron, 1992; Lo, Mamaysky & Wang,
  2000). The momentum and mean-reversion rules correspond to documented return
  continuation and reversal (Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993; De Bondt & Thaler, 1985).
  The RSI rule and its 30/70 thresholds follow Wilder (1978).
- **Optimising allocation given beliefs.** Mean–variance portfolio choice
  (Markowitz, 1952). Expected return and covariance are both expressed per step, as
  in Merton (1969). A forecast is spread over its horizon following Chiarella, Iori
  & Perelló (2009).
- **Behavioural preferences.** A seeded fraction of agents uses CPT (Tversky &
  Kahneman, 1992). It is applied to each asset separately — narrow framing
  (Barberis & Huang, 2001; Barberis, Huang & Thaler, 2006) — as a
  certainty-equivalent premium of holding the asset over holding cash. The gamble
  is evaluated over the forecast horizon, which plays the role of Benartzi &
  Thaler's (1995) evaluation period.
- **Reference points.** By default the agent's own average purchase price
  (Shefrin & Statman, 1985; Odean, 1998). Alternatives are peak wealth, a
  moving-average price, and initial wealth (Arkes et al., 2008; Baucells, Weber &
  Welfens, 2011).
- **Noise traders.** Random beliefs: an ad hoc rule, theoretically motivated by
  noise-trader models (De Long et al., 1990).
- **Buy-and-hold investors.** An ad hoc passive rule that serves as a control.
- **Risk management.** Fundamentalists liquidate a position on a stop-loss or
  take-profit trigger, a common heuristic (Kaminski & Lo, 2014).

#### II.i.c Why is a certain decision model chosen?

- **Separability.** Every mechanism is a separate module with its own switch, so
  its contribution can be isolated by controlled experiment. The mean–variance rule
  has a closed form, which keeps allocation transparent.
- **Theoretical considerations.** CPT is the best-established descriptive model of
  choice under risk (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Maximising CPT value directly over
  portfolio weights is ill-posed or yields corner solutions under power value
  functions (He & Zhou, 2011). The model therefore uses a hybrid: the CPT premium
  enters the mean–variance rule as the expected return. Risk is then priced twice —
  by CPT and by the covariance term — which is a deliberate, stated simplification.
  The premium is measured in log-return units, so it is commensurate with the
  covariance and the loss-aversion coefficient stays comparable with empirical
  estimates.
- **Reference to other studies.** The chartist–fundamentalist–noise ecology is the
  canonical heterogeneous-agent set-up and is known to generate stylised facts.
- **Pattern-oriented modelling.** Several stylised facts jointly constrain the
  mechanisms.
- **Data availability.** No individual-level trading data are available.
  Preference parameters therefore come from experimental literature, and the
  remaining parameters are calibrated to aggregate market data.
- **Microstructure.** A continuous order book makes realised prices genuine
  transaction prices. Spread, depth, order types and cancellations are known to
  contribute to return statistics.

#### II.i.d If the model or a submodel is based on empirical data, where does the data come from?

- **CPT parameters.** Laboratory experiments that elicited certainty equivalents
  (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992): $\lambda=2.25$, $\alpha=0.88$, probability weighting
  0.61 for gains and 0.69 for losses. The population uses the curvature and
  weighting values; the protagonist uses all four.
- **Calibration and validation targets.** Daily closing prices, converted to
  close-to-close log returns: S&P 500, NASDAQ-100 and FTSE 100 (March 2017 – March
  2026), and VinFast Auto (September 2021 – March 2026, labelled a recent stress
  window). The files are supplied by the user (the vendor still needs naming; Part
  C, point 10). The `convert-benchmarks` pipeline step converts them from Excel
  workbooks, and `config/benchmarks/manifest.csv` registers each with its SHA-256
  hash.
- **Institutional anchors.** The margin requirements are consistent with US
  Regulation T (50% initial margin) and FINRA Rule 4210 (30% maintenance margin on
  short equity positions). The tick size matches the one-cent (USD 0.01) minimum
  quoting increment of SEC Regulation NMS Rule 612.
- No surveys, interviews, participatory methods or individual trading records are
  used.

#### II.i.e At which level of aggregation were the data available?

- **CPT parameters:** individual choices in experiments, summarised as median
  estimates. The population dispersion used under heterogeneity is itself a
  calibration parameter.
- **Market data:** aggregate (index or single stock), daily frequency.

Calibration is therefore indirect: micro-level rules are fitted to macro-level
targets (Fagiolo, Moneta & Windrum, 2007; Grazzini & Richiardi, 2015).

### II.ii Individual decision making

#### II.ii.a What are the subjects and objects of decision making? On which level of aggregation is decision making modelled? Are multiple levels included?

The subjects are individual investors (the agents), each acting for one account.
The objects of decision are:

1. the allocation of wealth across the $n$ risky assets and cash, as target
   portfolio weights (fundamentalists, chartists, and the protagonist when running
   either rule);
2. for each asset, whether to trade, the side, the limit price, and the quantity
   (all types);
3. liquidating a position when a stop-loss or take-profit triggers
   (fundamentalists);
4. which single asset to trade this step (noise traders);
5. whether to keep buying toward a target weight (buy-and-hold investors).

Whether an order goes out as a market or a limit order is set on the agent's
behalf by a collector rule. It becomes a market order when the quote lies far from
the price — at least $\theta_{\mathrm{MO}}$ in log terms, read as a demand for
immediacy — or at random with probability $\rho_{\mathrm{MO}}$.

Decision making is modelled at one level, the individual. Exchange rules
(price–time priority, self-trade prevention, the collar) and broker rules (margin
checks, forced buy-ins) act top-down as constraints, not as decision makers.

#### II.ii.b What is the basic rationality behind agents' decision making? Do agents pursue an explicit objective?

Rationality is bounded: beliefs come from heuristics, and agents then optimise
given those beliefs.

- **Fundamentalists and chartists** (and a protagonist running either rule) pursue
  an explicit objective: the mean–variance optimal portfolio for their subjective
  per-step expected returns,
  $\mathbf w=(\rho_i\mathcal H_i)^{-1}\Sigma_t^{-1}\breve{\mathbf r}$, projected
  onto weight bounds and a gross-exposure cap. For CPT agents the forecast is first
  replaced by the CPT certainty-equivalent premium of holding over cash, so the
  objective embeds reference-dependent, loss-averse, probability-weighted
  valuation.
- **Noise traders** pursue no objective; they are random liquidity.
- **Buy-and-hold investors** follow a routine: accumulate until the risky share
  reaches $\omega_H$, then hold.

#### II.ii.c How do agents make their decisions?

A fundamentalist or chartist goes through these steps each time it is active
(formalism §4–5; `docs/agent_decision_flowchart.mmd`):

1. **Forecast** each asset's excess log return over its horizon. A fundamentalist
   uses $\hat r^F=\mathrm{clip}(\log(F/P);-1,1)$; a chartist uses its technical
   rule's signal.
2. **CPT transform**, if the agent is assigned CPT:
   $\breve r=\mathcal T_i(\hat r,\sigma_j;\varrho_{i,j})$, the certainty-equivalent
   premium of a moment-matched binomial return prospect over cash. Otherwise
   $\breve r=\hat r$.
3. **Joint Markowitz solve,**
   $\mathbf w=(\rho_i\mathcal H_i)^{-1}\Sigma_t^{-1}\breve{\mathbf r}$, then clamp
   the weights to their bounds and scale them to the gross-exposure cap.
4. **Target holdings,** $x^*=Ww/P$. The target is zero when a stop-loss or
   take-profit forces liquidation.
5. **Valuation quote.** A fundamentalist quotes $\tilde P=F\,e^{\kappa o}$; a
   chartist quotes $\tilde P=P\,e^{\breve r+\kappa o}$.
6. **No-trade band.** If $|\log(\tilde P/P)|\le\tau$ (plus a cost hurdle when that
   switch is on), the agent submits nothing in that asset. Otherwise it buys when
   $\tilde P>P$ and sells when $\tilde P<P$. A forced liquidation skips this test and
   always sells.
7. **Quantity.** Move toward $x^*$. Buys are capped by the running cash budget,
   sells by holdings (plus the short allowance, if any). Round the price to the
   tick — down for buys, up for sells — and check the band again.
8. **Order style.** The collector tags the order market or limit.

A **noise trader** draws $\zeta\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_N^2)$. The side is the sign
of $\zeta$, the quote is $P\,e^{\zeta}$, and the size is $\phi_N W/P^{\mathrm{lim}}$,
capped by cash for a buy and by holdings plus the short allowance for a sell. The
same no-trade band applies.

A **buy-and-hold investor** buys the gap to $(\omega_H/n)\,W/P$ at the current
price, rounded down to the tick, whenever it holds less than that.

The decision model is thus a utility-based choice for allocation followed by a
threshold rule set for order submission. Only the noise trader's choice is random.

#### II.ii.d Do the agents adapt their behaviour to changing endogenous and exogenous state variables? And if yes, how?

Yes, through state variables; the rules themselves never change.

- **Price history** drives technical signals: moving averages, RSI, breakout
  channels.
- **The latent fundamental (exogenous)** drives the fundamentalists' mispricing and
  quotes.
- **Realised volatility and co-movement** (the shared $\Sigma_t$) drive position
  sizes and the spread of the CPT prospect.
- **Own wealth** scales positions, because weights are fractions of wealth.
- **Own position history** sets the average entry price, which is the CPT
  reference point (paper gains and losses) and the stop-loss / take-profit anchor.
  Under CPT a large paper loss raises the premium and a large paper gain lowers it,
  so losers tend to be kept and winners realised: the direction of the disposition
  effect (formalism §7.5). When the prospect straddles the reference point the
  ranking is not guaranteed, consistent with prospect theory predicting the
  disposition effect only conditionally (Barberis & Xiong, 2009).
- **Peak wealth or a moving-average price** act the same way if those reference
  modes are selected.
- **Neighbours' opinions** shift quotes.
- **Cash and inventory** bound order sizes, and margin and borrow availability
  bound them again at execution.

Opinions do not respond to market outcomes. An opinion is a weighted average of
neighbours' lagged opinions plus a private signal drawn independently of prices.
Social influence therefore transmits sentiment between agents, but returns never
feed back into it (Part C, point 5).

#### II.ii.e Do social norms or cultural values play a role in the decision-making process?

No. The model has no norms, sanctions, reputation, or trust dynamics. Social
influence is imitation of neighbours' sentiment. The only trait governing it is
individual susceptibility: $\gamma_i\in[0.50,0.99]$ sets how far an agent defers to
neighbours rather than to its private information.

#### II.ii.f Do spatial aspects play a role in the decision process?

Only through the social space. The neighbourhood radius $r_{\mathrm{soc}}$ and the
distance-decaying weights decide whose opinions an agent takes into account.
Positions are fixed, and trading decisions have no spatial component.

#### II.ii.g Do temporal aspects play a role in the decision process?

- **Memory.** Moving averages over $h_S=5$ and $h_L=20$ steps; RSI over 14; the
  breakout channel over 20; the shared covariance over $v_\Sigma=50$; the
  moving-average reference over 20 (if selected); entry prices since the position
  opened; peak wealth over the whole history.
- **Forecast and evaluation horizon** $\mathcal H_i$, in steps. Sizing divides the
  forecast by it. CPT evaluates the gamble over it, with volatility
  $\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal H}$ while cash earns $\mathcal H\ln(1+r_f)$; this is
  Benartzi & Thaler's (1995) evaluation period. The reference configuration uses
  $\mathcal H=1$ provisionally.
- **Opinion lag.** Opinions use the previous step's values.
- **Order persistence.** A limit order rests across steps until it is filled,
  cancelled, or expires after 19 steps.
- **Discounting.** None of future utility. Cash compounds at $r_f$ (0 in the
  reference configuration). The allocation is recomputed every step without
  intertemporal hedging, so decisions are myopic.

#### II.ii.h To which extent and how is uncertainty included in the agents' decision rules?

- **Explicit risk.** Optimising agents trade expected return against risk through
  $\Sigma_t$. CPT agents also value an explicit subjective return distribution —
  a 13-scenario binomial ladder with variance $\sigma^2\mathcal H$ — through loss
  aversion and probability weighting, so tail scenarios receive distorted weight.
- **Stochastic elements** mimic unobserved heterogeneity and timing: noise-trader
  beliefs, private signals, random activation and arrival order, and random
  market-order choice.
- **Not represented:** uncertainty about execution (fill probability, queue
  position), estimation uncertainty in parameters, and strategic uncertainty about
  other agents.

### II.iii Learning

#### II.iii.a Is individual learning included in the decision process? How do individuals change their decision rules over time as a consequence of their experience?

No. Decision rules and their parameters are fixed for the run. Agents update state
— rolling statistics, entry prices, peak wealth, opinions — and that changes their
decisions, but they never revise rules, thresholds or preferences in response to
performance. The code keeps investor identity separate from strategy so that
strategy switching can be added later; it is not implemented.

#### II.iii.b Is collective learning implemented in the model?

No. There is no evolutionary selection, strategy imitation, genetic algorithm, or
fitness-based switching (in contrast with Brock & Hommes, 1998, and Lux &
Marchesi, 1999). Population shares are fixed by design, so the effect of a
behavioural mechanism is not confounded with endogenous changes in the strategy
mix.

### II.iv Individual sensing

#### II.iv.a What endogenous and exogenous state variables are individuals assumed to sense and consider in their decisions? Is the sensing process erroneous?

| Variable | Sensed by | Accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Own cash, holdings, wealth, entry prices, peak wealth | All agents | Exact |
| Market price history (last-trade prices) | All agents | Exact; public |
| Shared covariance matrix $\Sigma_t$ (trailing 50 log returns) | Fundamentalists, chartists | Computed exactly, but it is an estimate with finite-window sampling error |
| Latent fundamental value $F_{j,t}$ | Fundamentalists (including a fundamentalist protagonist) | Exact; no observation noise, and every fundamentalist sees the same value |
| Risk-free rate | Optimising agents | Exact |
| Own private signal $\eta_{i,t}$ | All agents, when social influence is on | Noisy by construction |

Agents do not observe the order book (best quotes, depth, queue position), other
agents' orders, holdings, or types.

#### II.iv.b What state variables of which other individuals can an individual perceive? Is the sensing process erroneous?

Only the previous step's opinions of its social neighbours (agents within
$r_{\mathrm{soc}}$), aggregated as a distance-weighted average. Transmission is
exact; the opinions themselves contain private noise. No other individual state is
perceived. Other agents' actions reach an agent only through prices.

#### II.iv.c What is the spatial scale of sensing?

Local (the network neighbourhood) for opinions. Global for market information —
prices and the covariance estimate — and, for fundamentalists, the fundamental
value.

#### II.iv.d Are the mechanisms by which agents obtain information modelled explicitly, or are individuals simply assumed to know these variables?

Market information is assumed to be known: it is public and free. Social
information is modelled explicitly, as averaging over neighbours' lagged opinions
in the network.

#### II.iv.e Are costs for cognition and costs for gathering information included in the model?

No. The only costs are transaction costs: 10 basis points per executed side,
paid by both buyer and seller. Agents anticipate them, as an enlarged no-trade
band, only when `transaction_cost_affects_decisions = 1`; that switch is off in
the reference configuration.

### II.v Individual prediction

#### II.v.a Which data does the agent use to predict future conditions?

Public market data: price history (chartists), the current gap between fundamental
value and price (fundamentalists), and the rolling covariance (risk). CPT agents
also use their own position history, through the reference point. Noise traders
do not predict: their beliefs are random. Buy-and-hold investors do not predict.

#### II.v.b What internal models are agents assumed to use to estimate future conditions or consequences of their decisions?

- **Fundamentalists** expect the log mispricing to close over $\mathcal H^F$ steps:
  $\hat r^F=\mathrm{clip}(\log(F/P);-1,1)$.
- **Chartists** use one of four rules:
  - momentum — the trend continues: $\log(\mathrm{MA}_5/\mathrm{MA}_{20})$, with a
    deadband $\theta_M$;
  - mean reversion — price returns to its long average:
    $\log(\mathrm{MA}_{20}/P)$;
  - RSI — oversold and overbought levels revert: $0.05\,(50-\mathrm{RSI})/50$;
  - breakout — a break out of the previous 20-step channel continues:
    $\log(P/H^{\mathrm{BO}})$ above it, $\log(P/L^{\mathrm{BO}})$ below it.
- **Risk model** (all optimising agents): per-step log returns with covariance
  $\Sigma_t$, independent across steps, so horizon variance is $\mathcal H\Sigma_t$.
  CPT agents represent the horizon return as a binomial approximation to a normal
  distribution with mean $\mathcal H\ell_f+\hat r$ and standard deviation
  $\sigma\sqrt{\mathcal H}$. The ladder is moment-matched, as in a
  Cox–Ross–Rubinstein tree (Cox, Ross & Rubinstein, 1979).
- **Quotes** are the price the agent expects at the end of its horizon — $F$ for a
  fundamentalist, $P\,e^{\breve r}$ for a chartist — shifted by sentiment
  $e^{\kappa o}$.

#### II.v.c Might agents be erroneous in the prediction process, and how is it implemented?

Yes, by construction:

- The forecast rules are heuristics that contradict each other and are often wrong
  ex post. Fundamentalists assume convergence that may not happen within
  $\mathcal H$; chartists extrapolate patterns that may be spurious.
- $\Sigma_t$ carries sampling error.
- Sentiment ($\kappa o$) moves quotes away from the strategy's valuation.
- Noise traders' beliefs are erroneous by design.
- CPT distorts the perceived prospect through probability weighting and loss
  aversion.

Agents do not learn from forecast errors (II.iii).

### II.vi Interaction

#### II.vi.a Are interactions among agents and entities assumed as direct or indirect?

- **Indirect, through the market.** Orders interact in the continuous double
  auction: resting limit orders provide liquidity, crossing orders consume it.
  Executed prices feed back into every agent's forecasts, covariance estimate and
  wealth. When short selling is on, margin calls and forced buy-ins pass losses on
  through the broker layer.
- **Direct, through social influence.** Each agent's opinion is a function of its
  neighbours' lagged opinions.

#### II.vi.b On what do the interactions depend?

- **Market interactions** depend on price compatibility (crossing), price–time
  priority, random arrival order, and feasibility rechecked at execution: buyer
  cash, seller inventory, margin, and borrowable supply. Self-trade prevention, the
  collar and a minimum economic fill also apply.
- **Social interactions** depend on distance in the social space (the radius and
  the $1/(1+d)$ weight) and on each agent's $\gamma_i$.
- **Agent type.** Every agent forms and transmits an opinion, but only
  fundamentalists and chartists (including a protagonist running either rule) use
  it in their quotes. Noise traders and buy-and-hold investors ignore their own
  opinion, yet their opinions still reach their neighbours.

#### II.vi.c If the interactions involve communication, how are such communications represented?

Implicitly. Each step, an agent reads a scalar opinion — an expected-return
signal — from each neighbour, with a one-step lag. There are no explicit messages,
no strategic communication, and no broadcasts.

#### II.vi.d If a coordination network exists, how does it affect the agent behaviour? Is the structure of the network imposed or emergent?

No coordination network exists. The social network is imposed: a static,
undirected, weighted random geometric graph generated at initialisation from
uniformly placed agents. It never rewires and does not depend on outcomes, so its
structure is not emergent. It is decentralised, with no central node or leader.

### II.vii Collectives

#### II.vii.a Do the individuals form or belong to aggregations that affect, and are affected by, the individuals? Are these aggregations imposed by the modeller or do they emerge during the simulation?

No. Agents do not form or belong to aggregations that act or hold their own state.
Strategy classes — fundamentalists, the chartist subtypes, noise traders,
buy-and-hold investors — are categories the modeller defines for parameterisation
and analysis. Locally correlated opinions may emerge in the social space, but they
are not represented as entities.

#### II.vii.b How are collectives represented?

Not applicable. Strategy classes appear only as labels, for example in the
per-strategy wealth statistics of the dashboard.

### II.viii Heterogeneity

#### II.viii.a Are the agents heterogeneous? If yes, which state variables and/or processes differ between the agents?

Yes.

- **At initialisation:**
  - social position, $\gamma_i$ and $\sigma_{i,\mathrm{priv}}$ for every agent;
  - the CPT flag, held by a random 70% of the anonymous population (the draw
    includes noise traders, for whom the flag has no effect; Part C, point 6);
  - CPT parameters per agent, if heterogeneity is enabled — it is off in the
    reference configuration, so all CPT agents share $\lambda$, $\alpha$ and $\psi$;
  - each chartist's technical rule;
  - each noise trader's private random stream.

  Anonymous agents receive identical endowments: 10,000 in cash and 100 shares of
  each asset. The protagonist's endowment can differ.
- **During the run,** cash, holdings, wealth, entry prices, peak wealth, opinions
  and trade statistics diverge endogenously.
- **Exchange test.** Suppose two agents of the same strategy class and CPT flag
  swap places at $t=0$, in a run with homogeneous preferences. The run changes only
  through their social profiles and their positions in the random sequences.
  Swapping agents across classes or CPT flags changes their decision models and
  therefore the outcome.

#### II.viii.b Are the agents heterogeneous in their decision making? If yes, which decision models or decision objects differ between the agents?

Yes. There are four strategy classes, with four technical rules among chartists,
plus CPT versus non-CPT valuation and the protagonist's individually set
parameters. Decision objects differ too:

- noise traders trade one randomly chosen asset per step;
- buy-and-hold investors only buy;
- fundamentalists are long-only;
- chartists and noise traders may sell short when short selling is enabled.

Within a class, rule parameters (windows, thresholds, risk aversion, bounds,
horizon) are common to all members.

### II.ix Stochasticity

#### II.ix.a What processes (including initialisation) are modelled by assuming they are random or partly random?

| Process | Distribution | When | Random stream |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assignment of strategy labels to ids | Random permutation | Initialisation | Agent stream (seed + 1) |
| Chartist rule (when the mode is `random`) | Uniform over the four rules | Initialisation | Agent stream |
| CPT assignment | Random subset of $\mathrm{round}(\varphi_{\mathrm{CPT}}N)$ agents | Initialisation | Dedicated stream derived from the seed, so changing $\varphi_{\mathrm{CPT}}$ leaves every other draw unchanged |
| CPT parameters (heterogeneity on) | Truncated normal for $\lambda$ (at most 10) and $\alpha^G,\alpha^L$ (at most 1.5); uniform for $\psi$, with $\psi^G=\psi^L$ | Initialisation | Agent stream; drawn for every agent |
| Social profile | Position uniform on the space; $\gamma_i\sim U[0.50,0.99]$; $\sigma_{i,\mathrm{priv}}\sim U[0.05,0.10]$ | Initialisation | Agent stream; drawn even when social influence is off |
| Fundamental innovations | Correlated standard normals | Warm-up and every step | Fundamental stream (seed) |
| Warm-up price noise | $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{warm}}^2)$ | Warm-up | Agent stream |
| Private signals | $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{i,\mathrm{priv}}^2)$ | Every step, every agent (social influence on) | Agent stream |
| Stale-order cancellation | Bernoulli, with probability rising in the order's distance from the price | Every step, each resting order | Agent stream |
| Activation order and activity | Random permutation; Bernoulli($\alpha_{\mathrm{act}}$) per agent | Every step | Agent stream |
| Market-order conversion | Bernoulli($\rho_{\mathrm{MO}}$) per order | Every step | Agent stream |
| Noise-trader asset choice and belief shock | Uniform over assets; $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_N^2)$ | When active | One private stream per noise trader |

Every engine is a 64-bit Mersenne Twister (`std::mt19937_64`) behind one wrapper, so
a configuration and its seed reproduce a run exactly. The randomness represents
fundamental news, idiosyncratic beliefs and the timing of trader arrival, and it
avoids artefacts of a fixed ordering. Keeping the streams separate has two
consequences: the fundamental path stays fixed when agent behaviour changes, and
noise-trader beliefs stay independent of the rest of the population.

Matching, price formation, the CPT valuation, portfolio optimisation, margin
enforcement, and the protagonist's preferences and rule are deterministic.

### II.x Observation

#### II.x.a What data are collected from the ABM for testing, understanding and analysing it, and how and when are they collected?

The simulator records every step; warm-up rows carry prices only. Each run is
archived in its own folder, named
`YYYYMMDDHHMMSS_<prospect_theory>_<prospect_heterogeneity>_<social>_<short_sale>_<n_F>_<n_C>_<n_N>_<n_BH>_<T>_<seed>`.

| Artefact | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| `asset_timeseries.csv` | Per step and asset: market and fundamental price; simple and log return; submitted limit and market orders; volume; trades; last trade price; best bid and ask; spread; midquote; depth; resting orders; five removal counters (stale, expired, infeasible resting, infeasible incoming, self-trade prevention) and collar rejections; transaction costs; realised and maximum allocative surplus and efficiency; mean agent portfolio weight |
| `step_timeseries.csv` | The primary asset's view, plus net demand, order imbalance, the fundamental signal and the wealth Gini coefficient |
| `trade_ledger.csv` (optional) | Every execution, with both counterparties, price, quantity and fees |
| `agents_final.csv` | Per agent: strategy, endowment, social profile, final opinion, cash, stock, trades, volume, wealth, costs, entry price, peak wealth, last reference point, CPT parameters, protagonist flag |
| `protagonist_timeseries.csv` | Every step: the protagonist's cash, stock, wealth and risky weight; cumulative trades, volume and costs; population mean and median wealth; the protagonist's wealth percentile |
| `summary_metrics.csv`, `stylised_facts.csv` | Headline scalars, and in-simulator screening per asset (moments; lag-1 autocorrelation of $r$, $\lvert r\rvert$ and $r^2$) |
| `config.json`, `config_used.cfg`, `metadata.json`, `logs/run.log` | The exact parameters, plus model version, git commit, dirty flag and compiler |
| `experiment_manifest.csv` | Append-only index of every run in an experiment |

The Python analysis layer (`abm_analysis`) never simulates. The same functions
feed the dashboard and the exported CSV tables, so the two cannot disagree. It
computes:

- **per asset:** Ljung–Box tests at lags 10, 20 and 30 (Ljung & Box, 1978);
  Jarque–Bera and excess kurtosis (Jarque & Bera, 1980); ARCH-LM at lags 10 and 20
  (Engle, 1982); GARCH(1,1) persistence (Bollerslev, 1986); autocorrelation of
  raw, absolute and squared returns; drawdown, volume, spread, allocative
  efficiency and the wealth Gini. Tests use a 5% significance level.
- **stress regimes:** the split described in I.ii.c, applied identically to
  simulated and empirical series.
- **across seeds:** stylised-fact reproduction rates, coefficients of variation,
  pairwise and k-sample distribution tests, ADF stationarity, and seed convergence
  (Law, 2015), each turned into a pass / borderline / fail verdict against fixed
  thresholds.
- **against benchmarks:** the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, normalised
  Wasserstein distance, errors at the 1, 5, 95 and 99% quantiles, and the RMSE
  between autocorrelation functions over 30 lags.

#### II.x.b What key results, outputs or characteristics of the model are emerging from the individuals?

Emergent outputs:

- each asset's price path and its deviation from fundamental value (mispricing,
  bubbles, crashes);
- the statistical properties of returns — tails, linear and nonlinear dependence —
  which are the stylised facts under test;
- stress episodes: volatility bursts and drawdowns;
- bid–ask spread, depth, volume, cancellations and order-flow composition;
- realised cross-asset return correlation, compared with the fundamental
  correlation;
- allocative efficiency;
- short squeezes and forced buy-ins, when short selling is enabled;
- the wealth distribution (Gini) and the protagonist's rank within it.

The fundamental process, the strategy mix, the decision rules and the institutional
rules are imposed, not emergent. Whether the stylised facts emerge is the
hypothesis under test, not an assumed property of the model.

## III. Details

### III.i Implementation details

#### III.i.a How has the model been implemented?

- **Simulation engine.** C++17, built with CMake (debug, release and sanitizer
  presets), with no third-party dependencies. Each domain is a static library —
  configuration, market, order book, behavioural finance, agents and strategies,
  simulation, metrics, output, archive — and dependencies flow strictly downward.
  The engine delegates every modelling assumption to a named collaborator: the
  activation scheduler, social model, order collector, clearing engine, risk-control
  service, and diagnostics recorder.
- **Analysis layer.** Python 3.11 or later: pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels,
  arch, Optuna, and a read-only FastAPI dashboard with Plotly charts. An optional
  PostgreSQL store uses SQLAlchemy and psycopg. The analysis layer launches the C++
  binary but never simulates.
- **Platform.** Developed and run on macOS (Apple M1 Pro, Apple clang 21).
  Continuous integration on GitHub Actions runs a warnings-as-errors build with
  CTest, a clang-format check, and pytest on every push.
- **Runtime.** Release build, single thread, reference configuration (301 agents,
  two assets, trade ledger on): 0.10 s for 25 + 100 steps and 4.1 s for
  25 + 5,000 steps, about 0.8 ms per step. The 5,000-step trade ledger is 39 MB.
- **Development time.** Not recoverable from the repository, whose history starts
  on 9 August 2026; the author should state it.

#### III.i.b Is the model accessible, and if so where?

The source code, configuration, formal specification and documentation are in the
GitHub repository `github.com/JustSimpleLucas/BABM`. Before publication, add a
licence and archive a tagged release with a persistent identifier, for example on
Zenodo or CoMSES Net. Empirical benchmark data are not in the repository (`data/`
is git-ignored); whether they can be redistributed depends on the vendor's licence.

### III.ii Initialisation

#### III.ii.a What is the initial state of the model world, i.e. at time $t=0$ of a simulation run?

- **Assets.** Two assets, `alpha` and `beta`, with $P_{j,0}=F_{j,0}=100$ and tick
  0.01. Both follow GBM with drift $\mu_F=0.0002$ per step; volatility
  $\sigma_F=0.0003$ (`alpha`) and $0.001$ (`beta`); $\rho_{AB}=0$; floor
  $F_{\min}=1$.
- **Population.** 100 fundamentalists; 100 chartists, each with a rule drawn
  uniformly (29 / 24 / 25 / 22 in the reference run); 100 noise traders; no
  buy-and-hold investors; and one fundamentalist protagonist. Strategy labels are
  shuffled before ids are assigned.
- **Endowment.** Every agent, protagonist included in the reference configuration,
  starts with 10,000 in cash and 100 shares of each asset. Initial wealth is valued
  at the end-of-warm-up prices (about 29,987 in the reference run), so the initial
  risky share is about two-thirds.
- **CPT.** Assigned at random to $\mathrm{round}(0.7\times300)=210$ anonymous agents
  (in the reference run 73 fundamentalists, 70 chartists, 67 noise traders), all
  sharing $\lambda=3.305$, $\alpha^G=\alpha^L=0.88$, $\psi^G=0.61$, $\psi^L=0.69$.
  The protagonist uses CPT with Tversky & Kahneman's (1992) values $\lambda=2.25$,
  $\alpha=0.88$, $\psi^G=0.61$, $\psi^L=0.69$.
- **Reference state.** Each endowed position's entry price is the last warm-up
  price; peak wealth equals initial wealth; the fallback reference point is
  $\nu^0=100$.
- **Social.** Positions uniform on the unit square; $\gamma_i\sim U[0.50,0.99]$;
  $\sigma_{\mathrm{priv}}\sim U[0.05,0.10]$; neighbour radius 0.4; all opinions 0.
- **Market.** Order books empty; cumulative statistics zero.
- **Price history.** 25 warm-up steps of a fundamental-pull process
  ($\beta_{\mathrm{warm}}=0.05$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{warm}}=0.005$), so indicators
  (longest window 20) and the covariance estimate exist from the first trading
  step. The validator rejects a warm-up shorter than the longest lookback of any
  active strategy, the protagonist's included.

#### III.ii.b Is initialisation always the same, or is it allowed to vary among simulations?

The structure is the same for a given configuration; the realisation varies by
seed. Social profiles, the chartist rule mix, the CPT assignment, heterogeneous CPT
draws, and the warm-up and fundamental paths all depend on it. A configuration and
its seed reproduce a run exactly, and experiments replicate across seeds (for
example `replicate --seeds 1:30`).

Enabling the protagonist adds one participant and therefore changes the agent-side
random stream, because social initialisation and activation draws scale with
population size. Disabling it reproduces pre-protagonist runs byte for byte.

#### III.ii.c Are the initial values chosen arbitrarily or based on data?

| Basis | Values |
| --- | --- |
| Normalisation or arbitrary choice | Initial prices (100); endowments (10,000 cash, 100 shares per asset); the unit social space; warm-up parameters |
| Literature | CPT curvature and probability weighting (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992); the protagonist's CPT parameters |
| Institutional anchors | Margin requirements (Regulation T, FINRA Rule 4210); tick size (Regulation NMS Rule 612) |
| Earlier calibration | Values carrying many decimals: $\lambda$, its dispersion, the heterogeneous $\psi$ interval, the noise-trader belief s.d. and order size, the market-order probability, the cancellation probability. They come from an Optuna study on an earlier model version and must be re-estimated (Part C, point 8). |
| Provisional | Forecast horizons (1); population mix (100 / 100 / 100 / 0) |

### III.iii Input data

#### III.iii.a Does the model use input from external sources such as data files or other models to represent processes that change over time?

No. Apart from the configuration, nothing is read during a run: the fundamental
value process is generated internally. Empirical series enter only offline, as
calibration and validation targets (II.i.d), and they are compared with simulated
returns rather than driving the simulation.

### III.iv Submodels

#### III.iv.a What, in detail, are the submodels that represent the processes listed in "Process overview and scheduling"?

The submodels follow the order of the schedule. Complete statements are in
`docs/formalism.tex`.

**1. Fundamental value (formalism §3.1–3.2).** Every asset advances in one joint
draw:

$$
F_{j,t+1}=\max\Bigl\{F_{\min,j},\;F_{j,t}\exp\bigl[(\mu_{F,j}-\tfrac12\sigma_{F,j}^2)+\sigma_{F,j}\,\zeta^F_{j,t}\bigr]\Bigr\},
\qquad
\zeta^F_{j,t}=a\,z_{j,t}+b\sum_k z_{k,t},
$$

where $a=\sqrt{1-\rho_{AB}}$, $b=\bigl(\sqrt{1+(n-1)\rho_{AB}}-a\bigr)/n$, and
$z_{j,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ are independent. The map is the closed-form one-factor
square root of the equicorrelation matrix. Unlike a Cholesky factorisation, it
stays exact at the positive-semi-definite boundary.

**2. Warm-up price process (§3.3).**
$r^{\mathrm{warm}}_{j,u}=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\beta_{\mathrm{warm}}(F_{j,u}-P_{j,u})/P_{j,u}+\xi_{j,u};-0.95,0.95\bigr)$,
with $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{warm}})$, and
$P_{j,u+1}=P_{j,u}(1+r^{\mathrm{warm}}_{j,u})$. It is used only to seed history.

**3. Cash accrual.** $c_{i,t}\leftarrow c_{i,t}(1+r_f)$.

**4. Opinion dynamics (§5.1–5.4).**

$$
S_{i,t}=\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal N_i}\omega_{il}\,o_{l,t-1}}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal N_i}\omega_{il}},
\qquad
o_{i,t}=\gamma_i S_{i,t}+(1-\gamma_i)\,\eta_{i,t},
\qquad
\eta_{i,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{i,\mathrm{priv}}^2),
$$

where $\mathcal N_i=\{l\neq i:d_{il}\le r_{\mathrm{soc}}\}$ and
$\omega_{il}=1/(1+d_{il})$. An agent with no neighbours has $S_{i,t}=0$. With
social influence off, $o_{i,t}\equiv0$.

**5. Order lifecycle (§6.2).** Each book removes resting orders past their expiry
step (creation step + 19). It then cancels each surviving order with probability

$$
\min\Bigl\{1,\;\rho_0+\gamma_{\mathrm{mis}}\Bigl|\log\frac{P^{\mathrm{lim}}}{P_{j,t}}\Bigr|\Bigr\}.
$$

**6. Activation.** A fresh random permutation of all agents is drawn; each agent is
active with probability $\alpha_{\mathrm{act}}$.

**7. Risk estimate (§4.1).**

$$
\Sigma_{t,jk}=\frac{1}{T_t-1}\sum_{u=t-T_t+1}^{t}(\ell_{j,u}-\bar\ell_j)(\ell_{k,u}-\bar\ell_k),
$$

with $T_t=\min\{v_\Sigma,\text{common return history}\}$, warm-up included. Before
the Cholesky solve the matrix gets a variance floor and, if needed, escalating
diagonal loading; a diagonal solve is the fallback.

**8. Forecasts (§4.3–4.5).**

- **Fundamentalist:** $\hat r^F_{i,j,t}=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\log(F_{j,t}/P_{j,t});-1,1\bigr)$.
  A long position is liquidated when
  $\pi_{i,j,t}=(P_{j,t}-\bar P^{\mathrm{entry}}_{i,j,t})/\bar P^{\mathrm{entry}}_{i,j,t}\le-\theta^{SL}$
  or $\ge\theta^{TP}$.
- **Momentum chartist:** $\mathrm{clip}(g^M;-1,1)$ when $|g^M|>\theta_M$, else 0,
  where $g^M=\log\bigl(\mathrm{MA}^{(h_S)}/\mathrm{MA}^{(h_L)}\bigr)$.
- **Mean-reversion chartist:** $\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\log(\mathrm{MA}^{(h_L)}/P);-1,1\bigr)$.
- **RSI chartist:** $\mathrm{clip}\bigl(0.05\,(50-\mathrm{RSI})/50;-1,1\bigr)$, with RSI
  over 14 steps.
- **Breakout chartist:** $\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\log(P/H^{\mathrm{BO}})\bigr)$ above the
  previous 20-step high, $\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\log(P/L^{\mathrm{BO}})\bigr)$ below the
  previous low, 0 inside the channel. The channel excludes the current price.

**9. CPT transform (§7).** It applies to CPT-assigned fundamentalists and chartists.

*Scenario ladder.* Write $n_{\mathrm{CPT}}=2m+1$ and $s=0,\dots,2m$. Then

$$
p_s=\binom{2m}{s}2^{-2m},
\qquad
\Delta=\frac{2\min\{\sigma_{j,t}\sqrt{\mathcal H_i},\,0.95\}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{CPT}}-1}},
\qquad
R_s=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\mathcal H_i\ell_f+\hat r+(s-m)\Delta;-0.95,0.95\bigr),
\qquad
x_s=R_s-\varrho_{i,j,t},
$$

where $\ell_f=\log(1+r_f)$, $\sigma_{j,t}=\sqrt{\Sigma_{t,jj}}$, and $\hat r$ is first
clipped to $\pm0.95$. The ladder has mean $\mathcal H_i\ell_f+\hat r$ and variance
$\sigma^2\mathcal H_i$ for any $n_{\mathrm{CPT}}$, so the scenario count sets only
the resolution of the tails.

*Value and weighting functions.*

$$
v_i(x)=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha_i^G}, & x\ge0,\\ -\lambda_i(-x)^{\alpha_i^L}, & x<0,\end{cases}
\qquad
w_i^d(p)=\frac{p^{\psi_i^d}}{\bigl(p^{\psi_i^d}+(1-p)^{\psi_i^d}\bigr)^{1/\psi_i^d}},\quad d\in\{G,L\}.
$$

Gains are ranked from best to worst and losses from worst to best. The decision
weights are $\pi_k=w(\sum_{h\le k}p_h)-w(\sum_{h<k}p_h)$, and
$\mathcal V^{\mathrm{hold}}=\sum_k\pi_k\,v_i(x_k)$.

*Certainty-equivalent premium over cash.* Over the horizon, cash returns
$\mathcal H_i\ell_f$ for certain. So

$$
\breve r_{i,j,t}=\mathrm{clip}\Bigl(v_i^{-1}\bigl(\mathcal V^{\mathrm{hold}}_{i,j,t}\bigr)+\varrho_{i,j,t}-\mathcal H_i\ell_f;\;-1,1\Bigr).
$$

*Reference point.* $\varrho_{i,j,t}=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\log(\nu_{i,j,t}/B_{i,j,t});-0.95,0.95\bigr)$.
The anchor $\nu$ is the agent's average entry price by default. Divide by
$B=P_{j,t}$ for price anchors (entry price, moving average, configured value) and
by $B=W_{i,t}$ for wealth anchors (peak or initial wealth).

*Properties, each pinned by a unit test:*

- the sign of the premium is the hold-versus-cash choice;
- a riskless forecast passes through unchanged;
- the premium is homogeneous of degree one in return units;
- under reflection — a paper loss or gain larger than the spread of the prospect —
  a paper loss raises the premium and a paper gain lowers it, which is the
  direction of the disposition effect.

**10. Portfolio allocation (§4.2).**

$$
\mathbf w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{\rho_i\mathcal H_i}\,\Sigma_t^{-1}\breve{\mathbf r}_{i,t},
\qquad
w^{(1)}_{i,j,t}=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(w^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,j,t};\underline w_i,\overline w_i\bigr),
\qquad
w_{i,j,t}=
\begin{cases}
w^{(1)}_{i,j,t}, & \Lambda_i\le G_i,\\
\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\tfrac{G_i}{\Lambda_i}w^{(1)}_{i,j,t};\underline w_i,\overline w_i\bigr), & \Lambda_i>G_i,
\end{cases}
$$

where $\Lambda_i=\sum_j|w^{(1)}_{i,j,t}|$. Fundamentalists use bounds $[0,1]$.
Chartists use $[\max\{\underline w^{\mathrm{cfg}},-\bar w^{\mathrm{short}}\},\overline w^{\mathrm{cfg}}]$,
which is $[0,1]$ when short selling is off. The target holding is
$x^*_{i,j,t}=W_{i,t}w_{i,j,t}/P_{j,t}$.

**11. Quotes, side, size and style (§4.4–4.7, §5.5, §6.1).**

$$
\tilde P^F_{i,j,t}=F_{j,t}\,e^{\kappa o_{i,t}},
\qquad
\tilde P^C_{i,j,t}=P_{j,t}\,e^{\breve r^C_{i,j,t}+\kappa o_{i,t}},
\qquad
\tilde P^N_{i,j,t}=P_{j,t}\,e^{\mathrm{clip}(\zeta_{i,t};-20,20)},\quad \zeta_{i,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_N^2).
$$

- **No-trade band.** No order is submitted when
  $|\log(\tilde P/P)|\le\tau^{\mathrm{eff}}=\tau+\iota^D\log\frac{1+f_{\mathrm{tc}}}{1-f_{\mathrm{tc}}}$.
  The band is checked again after tick rounding.
- **Side.** A fundamentalist or chartist buys when $\tilde P>P$ and sells when
  $\tilde P<P$. A noise trader follows the sign of $\zeta$.
- **Quantity.** Buys are $\min\{(x^*-x)^+,\ \tilde c/(P^{\mathrm{lim}}(1+f_{\mathrm{tc}}))\}$,
  where $\tilde c$ is the running cash budget. Sells are $(x-x^*)^+$, or the whole
  position under forced liquidation. A noise trader's size is
  $\min\{\phi_NW/P^{\mathrm{lim}},\ \text{cash cap or holdings plus short cap}\}$.
  A buy-and-hold investor buys $\min\{(\tfrac{\omega_H}{n}\tfrac{W}{P}-x)^+,\ \text{cash cap}\}$.
- **Tick rounding.** Buys round down, $\delta\lfloor\tilde P/\delta\rfloor$; sells round
  up, $\delta\lceil\tilde P/\delta\rceil$. Rounding therefore never makes an order more
  aggressive than intended.
- **Style.** An order becomes a market order when
  $|\log(P^{\mathrm{lim}}/P)|\ge\theta_{\mathrm{MO}}$ or a uniform draw falls below
  $\rho_{\mathrm{MO}}$; otherwise it is a limit order.

**12. Matching and accounting (§6.3–6.6).**

- Each asset's book uses price–time priority, and a trade executes at the resting
  order's limit price $p$.
- If the best opposing order belongs to the same agent, that resting order is
  cancelled instead of traded (self-trade prevention).
- A market order is rejected when it would execute beyond the collar
  $\theta_{\mathrm{col}}$. A collar of 0 disables the check.
- The matched quantity is
  $m=\min\{q^{\mathrm{rem}}_u,\,q^{\mathrm{rem}}_r,\,c_b/(p(1+f_{\mathrm{tc}})),\,\bar q^S_s(p)\}$.
  It uses the buyer's actual cash and the seller's inventory, plus short capacity
  limited by initial margin and the remaining borrow pool when short selling is
  enabled.
- A fill executes only if $m>\max\{10^{-8},\,10^{-6}\min(q^{\mathrm{rem}}_u,q^{\mathrm{rem}}_r)\}$.
- Accounting: $c_b\leftarrow c_b-mp(1+f_{\mathrm{tc}})$,
  $c_s\leftarrow c_s+mp(1-f_{\mathrm{tc}})$, $x_{b,j}\leftarrow x_{b,j}+m$,
  $x_{s,j}\leftarrow x_{s,j}-m$. Fees leave the economy; nobody receives them.
- Market-order residuals are cancelled; limit-order residuals rest in the book.
  Entry prices, trade counts and the ledger update at each fill.

**13. Price formation (§6.7).** $P_{j,t+1}=\max\{\epsilon_P,P^{\mathrm{last}}_{j,t}\}$
when asset $j$ traded in the step, otherwise $P_{j,t+1}=P_{j,t}$; the log return
is $\ell_{j,t+1}=\log(P_{j,t+1}/P_{j,t})$. Each asset is resolved independently.

**14. Maintenance margin and forced buy-in (§6.9).** This applies only when short
selling is enabled. Let $s$ be the short position and $E=c+xP_{j,t+1}$ the equity
in that asset. If $E/(sP_{j,t+1})<M^S_{\min}$, the agent is bought in for

$$
b=\min\Bigl\{\bigl(s-E/(M^S_0P_{j,t+1})\bigr)^+,\;s,\;c/\bigl(P_{j,t+1}(1+f^B)\bigr)\Bigr\}
$$

shares at $P_{j,t+1}$, against an external counterparty. Buy-ins happen after the
price for the step is formed and do not move it.

**15. Diagnostics (§6.6, §6.8).**

- Allocative efficiency $AE_{j,t}=AS_{j,t}/MS_{j,t}$, treating limit prices as
  reservation values (Smith, 1962; Gode & Sunder, 1993).
- Cross-sectional mean portfolio weights.
- The wealth Gini coefficient.

#### III.iv.c How were submodels designed or chosen, and how were they parameterised and then tested?

**Design.** Every submodel follows the literature cited in II.i. `docs/formalism.tex`
is maintained alongside the code as a formal specification, and "formula note"
comments in the source restate each equation where it is implemented. Parameters
live in one configuration file and are validated before any run: unknown keys are
rejected, and constraints that span several keys are checked.

**Parameterisation**, in three tiers:

1. **Literature and institutional values:** CPT curvature and probability
   weighting, margins, the tick size.
2. **Normalisations and modelling choices:** prices, endowments, the social space,
   windows.
3. **Simulated minimum-distance calibration** with Optuna's tree-structured Parzen
   estimator (Bergstra et al., 2011; Akiba et al., 2019), over 12 parameters: the
   CPT fraction, $\lambda$, $\sigma_\lambda$, the numbers of fundamentalists and
   chartists, the activity rate, $\sigma_N$, $\phi_N$, $\rho_{\mathrm{MO}}$,
   $\rho_0$, $\mathcal H^F$ and $\mathcal H^C$.
   - **Objective.** For each benchmark, the score sums the KS statistic, the
     normalised Wasserstein distance, the tail-quantile error, the ACF RMSE for
     absolute and squared returns, and half the ACF RMSE for raw returns. The
     benchmark scores are averaged. A penalty of 0.75 is added for each failed
     stylised-fact test (autocorrelation, fat tails, volatility clustering). In a
     multi-asset run each asset is scored and the scores averaged.
   - **Selection.** The best candidates are re-run on at least 5 validation seeds
     (10 recommended) and ranked by fit × seed-robustness pass rate. The winner is
     then checked against benchmark series held out of the search. The budget is at
     most 300 runs.
   - **Status.** The calibrated values now shipped predate the October 2026
     corrections and must be re-estimated (Part C, point 8).

**Testing.**

- **C++:** 174 unit and integration test functions across 15 test files. They
  cover:
  - CPT property tests: the moment-matched ladder; the inverse value function;
    premium sign equals the hold-versus-cash choice; the risk-free rate as the cash
    benchmark; riskless pass-through; scenario count setting only resolution;
    return-unit homogeneity; reflection implying the disposition direction; and an
    end-to-end test in which a CPT fundamentalist keeps a loser and realises a
    winner;
  - the order book: price–time priority, partial fills, cancellation, self-trade
    prevention;
  - the portfolio optimiser, each strategy, the risk controls, configuration
    parsing and validation, and the output schema;
  - complete simulation runs.
- **Python:** 207 test functions, including a cross-language test that the C++ CSV columns,
  the Python required columns and the database columns agree, and tests of the
  repository structure.
- **Regression baselines.** For a fixed configuration and seed, the output's SHA-256
  hash reproduces bit for bit. Deliberate behaviour changes are documented with
  before-and-after evidence in `docs/baselines.md` — for example, a forecast horizon
  of 1 reproduces the pre-change output byte for byte.
- **Sanitizers and CI.** AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer builds for
  memory-sensitive code. Continuous integration on every push: a warnings-as-errors
  build with CTest, a clang-format check, and pytest.
- **Analysis of submodels.**
  - *Mechanism contrasts.* The mechanism switches (CPT, heterogeneity, social
    influence, short selling) and the population mix are encoded in the run id, and
    the dashboard compares them as conditions across seeds.
  - *Seed robustness.* Verdicts use fixed thresholds: a coefficient of variation of
    at most 0.10 passes and at most 0.25 is borderline; a stylised-fact success rate
    of at least 80% passes and at least 60% is borderline. Verdicts require at least
    5 seeds.

Two risky assets with latent fundamentals; one LOB per asset; orders; broker risk-control rules; a synthetic 2-D social space with a static network; global state (time, $r_f$, shared covariance). |
| I.ii.b | Attributes (state variables, parameters) | Agents: id, strategy and rule, cash, holdings per asset, wealth, endowment, average entry price per asset, peak wealth, CPT flag and parameters ($\lambda,\alpha^G,\alpha^L,\psi^G,\psi^L$), last reference point, social coordinates, $\gamma_i$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{priv}}$, opinion, trade count, volume and costs; rule parameters per class. Assets: $F$, $P$, histories, GBM parameters, tick, supply, short interest; books: resting orders. Units: currency (prices start at 100), fractional shares, per-step log returns, steps. |
| I.ii.c | Exogenous drivers | Correlated GBM fundamental innovations (no jumps or regimes); a constant risk-free rate; institutional settings fixed per run. Crises are not imposed: stress regimes are identified after the run (top-quintile rolling volatility, or a drawdown of at least 10%). |
| I.ii.d | How is space included? | Not geographic. Fixed positions in a synthetic unit-square social space define the network (radius 0.4, weights $1/(1+d)$). Markets are aspatial. |
| I.ii.e | Temporal and spatial resolution and extent | Discrete steps of one trading period, read as one trading day when compared with daily data. Matching is continuous within a step; prices are recorded per step. 25 warm-up steps plus $T$ analysed steps (100 in the demonstration file, 50,000 in the regression baseline). Tick 0.01. Network mean degree 102 of 300. |
| I.iii.a | Who does what, in what order | Each step: cash accrual → joint fundamental draw → synchronous opinion update → order expiry and cancellation → random activation order → covariance estimate → order generation from start-of-step information → sequential continuous matching in activation order → last-trade price → wealth refresh, margin calls and forced buy-ins → recording. |
| II.i.a | Underlying concepts; link to complexity | Heterogeneous-agent market models; noise-trader risk and limits to arbitrage; short-sale constraints; order-driven microstructure; GBM fundamentals; herding as opinion pooling. Stylised facts are treated as emergent macro patterns and assessed jointly (pattern-oriented modelling). |
| II.i.b | Assumptions of the decision model | Bounded rationality: heuristic forecasts (fundamental reversion, four technical rules) combined with optimising mean–variance allocation. CPT for a seeded fraction of agents: narrow framing, a certainty-equivalent premium over cash, the horizon as evaluation period, purchase price as reference. Noise and buy-and-hold rules are ad hoc. |
| II.i.c | Why this decision model | Transparent, separable, switchable mechanisms; the heterogeneous-agent ecology is known to generate stylised facts; CPT is the leading descriptive model of risky choice; direct CPT optimisation is ill-posed (He & Zhou, 2011); an LOB makes prices transaction prices; no individual trading data are available. |
| II.i.d | Source of empirical data | CPT parameters from laboratory experiments (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Daily closing prices: S&P 500, NASDAQ-100 and FTSE 100 (2017–2026) and VinFast (2021–2026), user-supplied files registered with hashes. Institutional rules: Regulation T, FINRA Rule 4210, Regulation NMS Rule 612. |
| II.i.e | Aggregation level of the data | CPT: individual experimental choices summarised as medians. Market: aggregate daily series. Hence indirect calibration. |
| II.ii.a | Subjects and objects of decisions; levels | Individual investors decide portfolio weights and, per asset, whether to trade, the side, limit price and quantity. Fundamentalists also decide stop-loss and take-profit liquidation; noise traders decide which asset to trade. A collector rule sets market or limit style. One level of decision making; exchange and broker rules are constraints. |
| II.ii.b | Basic rationality; objectives | Bounded: heuristic beliefs with an explicit mean–variance objective, CPT-adjusted for CPT agents. Noise traders have no objective; buy-and-hold investors follow a target routine. |
| II.ii.c | How decisions are made | Forecast → CPT premium (if assigned) → joint Markowitz solve with bounds and a gross-exposure cap → target holdings → valuation quote ($Fe^{\kappa o}$ or $Pe^{\breve r+\kappa o}$) → no-trade band → side → quantity within cash and holdings → tick rounding → market or limit. Noise traders: a random belief shock sets side and price. |
| II.ii.d | Adaptation to state variables | Yes, through state, not rules: price history, the fundamental, covariance, wealth, entry price as reference point (disposition direction), neighbours' opinions, cash and inventory. Opinions receive no feedback from prices. |
| II.ii.e | Social norms, cultural values | None. Social influence is imitation of neighbours' sentiment, with individual susceptibility $\gamma_i$. |
| II.ii.f | Spatial aspects | Only through the social neighbourhood and distance weights. |
| II.ii.g | Temporal aspects | Memory windows (moving averages 5 and 20, RSI 14, breakout 20, covariance 50); forecast horizon as evaluation period; one-step opinion lag; order expiry after 19 steps; entry-price and peak-wealth memory; no discounting ($r_f=0$). |
| II.ii.h | Uncertainty in decision rules | Explicit: covariance-based risk, and for CPT agents a moment-matched return distribution valued with loss aversion and probability weighting. Stochastic beliefs, signals, activation and order style. Execution risk is not considered. |
| II.iii.a | Individual learning | None. Rules and parameters are fixed; only states update. |
| II.iii.b | Collective learning | None. Strategy shares are fixed by design. |
| II.iv.a | State variables sensed; errors | Own balance sheet; public prices; the shared covariance estimate. Fundamentalists observe the true fundamental without error; private signals are noisy. Agents do not see the order book or others' holdings. |
| II.iv.b | Other individuals perceived | Neighbours' lagged opinions, transmitted exactly. |
| II.iv.c | Spatial scale of sensing | Local for opinions; global for prices, covariance and the fundamental. |
| II.iv.d | Information mechanisms explicit or assumed | Market information is assumed known; social averaging is modelled explicitly. |
| II.iv.e | Costs of cognition and information | None. Only transaction costs exist, and they enter decisions only when that switch is on. |
| II.v.a | Data used for prediction | Price history, the fundamental gap, the rolling covariance, own reference point. |
| II.v.b | Internal prediction models | The fundamental gap closes over $\mathcal H^F$; trend, reversion, RSI and breakout extrapolation; independent per-step returns with covariance $\Sigma$. CPT agents use a binomial approximation of $\mathcal N(\mathcal H\ell_f+\hat r,\sigma^2\mathcal H)$. |
| II.v.c | Errors in prediction | Heuristic forecasts, sampling error in $\Sigma$, sentiment bias, noise-trader beliefs, CPT distortion. No learning from errors. |
| II.vi.a | Direct or indirect interaction | Indirect through the LOB, with prices feeding back; direct through opinion exchange. |
| II.vi.b | What interactions depend on | Price compatibility, price–time priority, random arrival order and feasibility; social distance and $\gamma_i$; agent type. |
| II.vi.c | Representation of communication | An implicit scalar opinion with a one-step lag. |

| II.vii.a | Collectives | None. Strategy classes are categories, not entities. |
| II.vii.b | Representation of collectives | Not applicable. |

| II.viii.b | Heterogeneity in decision making | Four classes (with four technical rules), CPT versus non-CPT valuation, and the protagonist. Parameters are common within a class. |
| II.ix.a | Stochastic processes | Initialisation: id shuffle, chartist rule, CPT assignment, CPT draws, social profiles. ## Part C — Points to resolve before using this text

These are places where the code, the documentation and the thesis framing
disagree, or where this document had to state an interpretation. None changes the
description above, which follows the code.

1. **The time unit is implicit.** Nothing states how long a step is, but
   calibration compares raw per-step log returns with daily close-to-close returns
   (`statistics/comparisons.py`; the KS statistic is not rescaled). That
   implicitly makes one step one trading day. On that reading the shipped
   fundamental volatilities (0.03% and 0.1% per step) are an order of magnitude
   below the benchmarks' daily volatilities, computed from the benchmark files:
   S&P 500 1.18%, NASDAQ-100 1.47%, FTSE 100 1.15%, VinFast 7.23%. State the
   mapping explicitly in the thesis.
2. **The strategy volatility windows are inert.** `fundamentalist_volatility_window`,
   `chartist_volatility_window` and `protagonist_volatility_window` never reach the
   order path. `generateOrders` takes $\sigma$ from the shared covariance diagonal
   (`covariance_window` = 50). The windows feed only the legacy single-order
   `generateOrder` path, which the simulation does not call, and the warm-up
   validator. `docs/config_reference.md` describes them as the window "used for
   realised volatility". Either route them into the order path or document them as
   legacy.
3. **The mispricing threshold does not govern trading.**
   `fundamentalist_mispricing_threshold` (and the protagonist's) sets the
   buy/sell/hold signal, but the active order path uses only that signal's
   forced-liquidation flag. Side and the no-trade decision come from the valuation
   quote and $\tau$, as formalism §4.5 states. The reference text "minimum relative
   deviation … before a fundamentalist trades" overstates its role.
4. **Two social parameters are described wrongly.**
   - `social_hold_tolerance` is a log-price no-trade band. It applies to
     fundamentalists, chartists and noise traders whether or not
     `social_enabled = 1` (`OrderCollector.cpp`). It is not an opinion deadband.
   - `social_opinion_kappa` scales how far an opinion moves the quote. How far
     neighbours move the opinion is set by $\gamma_i$.

   Correct both descriptions in `ConfigKeyRegistry.cpp`, regenerate
   `config_reference.md`, and update `config_guide.md`.
5. **Social influence has no market feedback.** Opinions are a spatially
   correlated noise process: neighbours' lagged opinions plus a private signal
   unrelated to prices. If the thesis calls this herding, say that agents herd on
   sentiment, not on observed returns or trades.
6. **CPT is assigned to agents for whom it has no effect.** The CPT subset is drawn
   from the whole anonymous population, so noise traders and buy-and-hold investors
   can be "CPT agents" with no consequence. The realised CPT share among optimising
   agents therefore varies with the seed: 73% of fundamentalists and 70% of
   chartists in the reference run. Report the realised share, or stratify the draw
   by strategy.
7. **The protagonist is partly sampled.** `config_guide.md` says nothing about the
   protagonist is sampled, yet the protagonist's social position, $\gamma$ and
   private-signal s.d. are drawn from the shared stream like everyone else's
   ($\gamma=0.71$ in the reference run). With social influence on, they shift the
   protagonist's quotes. Either make them configurable or say so in the case study.
8. **The calibrated values are stale.** $\lambda=3.305$, $\sigma_\lambda$, the
   $\psi$ interval, $\sigma_N$, $\phi_N$, $\rho_{\mathrm{MO}}$ and $\rho_0$ come from
   a calibration of an earlier model version. The CPT correction of 4 October 2026
   alone changes the meaning of $\lambda$ (see `config_guide.md` and
   `baselines.md`). Re-run the calibration before reporting calibrated values in
   III.iv.b.
9. **Some wording predates forecast horizons.** The opening of formalism §7 and the
   CPT section of `config_guide.md` still describe the premium as holding the asset
   "for one step" over "next step's" return. With $\mathcal H>1$ it is over the
   forecast horizon. The two readings coincide at the shipped $\mathcal H=1$.
10. **Provenance and access are incomplete.**
    - The benchmark manifest gives the source only as a user-supplied local
      dataset; name the vendor and retrieval date.
    - The repository has no licence file; add one and archive a tagged release
      with a DOI.
    - Development time cannot be inferred from git, whose history starts on
      9 August 2026.
